The Solar System: A Quick Guide

The Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a giant cloud of gas
and dust. At its center is the Sun, a star that contains more than 99% of the
Solar System's total mass. The Sun's gravity holds everything in orbit.

There are eight planets. In order from the Sun they are: Mercury, Venus, Earth,
Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. The first four are small, rocky
"terrestrial" planets. The last four are large "gas giants" and "ice giants".

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It has almost no
atmosphere, so its temperature swings wildly between day and night.

Venus is the hottest planet because of a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide
that traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect. Surface temperatures reach
about 465 degrees Celsius.

Earth is the only planet known to support life. About 71% of its surface is
covered by water, and its atmosphere is rich in nitrogen and oxygen.

Mars is known as the Red Planet because of iron oxide (rust) on its surface.
It has the tallest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, which is about
22 kilometers high.

Jupiter is the largest planet. It is a gas giant with a famous storm called
the Great Red Spot that has raged for hundreds of years. Jupiter has at least
95 known moons.

Saturn is famous for its spectacular ring system, made mostly of ice and rock.
Saturn is the least dense planet; it would float in water if you had a big
enough bathtub.

Uranus is an ice giant that rotates on its side, likely due to a massive
collision long ago. It appears blue-green because of methane in its atmosphere.

Neptune is the farthest planet from the Sun and has the strongest winds in the
Solar System, reaching over 2,000 kilometers per hour.

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, home to dwarf planets such as Pluto.
Pluto was reclassified from a planet to a dwarf planet in 2006.
